# Section 119 (Part 16): Architect-Level Question Bank

§118 covered senior-tier questions requiring diagnostic synthesis on a single host or incident. This section raises the bar to the **architect tier**: questions requiring fleet-scale and organizational thinking, explicit trade-off analysis across competing concerns — cost, reliability, security, compliance — and designing systems and policies rather than fixing one machine. There usually isn't a single correct design here; the interviewer is evaluating how the trade-offs are reasoned through and communicated.

## 1. Storage and Data Architecture at Scale

**Q: Design a backup strategy for a mixed fleet where some hosts hold regulated data subject to a strict retention requirement and others hold disposable, easily-regenerated cache data. How do you avoid over- or under-engineering either category?**

Model answer: I'd explicitly refuse to apply one uniform backup policy to both categories, since doing so either wastes budget and retention infrastructure on data that doesn't need it, or under-protects data that legally must be retained. For regulated data, I'd design around the actual retention and immutability requirements first — write-once/tamper-evident storage where required, a retention period matching the regulation rather than a generic "30 days," and documented, testable restore procedures, since an audit doesn't just check that backups exist but that they're demonstrably restorable (echoing the compliance-audit sampling pattern from §110). For disposable cache data, I'd deliberately choose a cheap, short-retention, best-effort backup posture — or no backup at all if regeneration is genuinely fast and reliable — and put the saved budget toward the regulated tier instead. The architectural discipline is tagging data by category at the source and routing it to the correct policy automatically, rather than relying on manual, error-prone per-host judgment calls about which policy applies.

What this probes for: Explicit recognition that uniform policy is itself a design mistake at this tier, and connecting backup design to compliance evidentiary requirements, not just technical restore capability (§79, §106, §110).

**Q: How would you design a network storage (NFS) architecture to avoid it becoming a fleet-wide single point of failure, given that NFS I/O waits are uninterruptible and can cascade into load spikes across every client?**

Model answer: I'd treat the NFS server's availability as a first-class architectural concern rather than an assumed constant, since a single NFS server going unresponsive doesn't just fail — it hangs every client process with an open handle to it in uninterruptible sleep, which can cascade into load-average spikes and effective unresponsiveness across the entire dependent fleet simultaneously (the exact mechanism behind the high-load/low-CPU diagnostic pattern from §118). Mitigations include redundant NFS serving (HA pairs with failover, or a clustered/distributed filesystem instead of a single NFS head), mount options that bound how long clients wait before giving up (`soft` mounts with a defined timeout, traded consciously against `hard` mounts' stronger consistency guarantee), and reducing the blast radius by not making every service in the fleet depend on the same single NFS export for unrelated purposes. I'd also make sure monitoring specifically watches NFS server health and client-side hang symptoms as leading indicators, since by the time load average has spiked fleet-wide, the incident is already fully underway.

What this probes for: Treating a shared storage dependency as a systemic single point of failure at fleet scale, and connecting the technical mechanism (uninterruptible sleep) to the architectural mitigation, not just naming HA as a generic answer (§78, §81).

**Q: A team proposes moving from LVM snapshots to a full storage-tier redesign using thin provisioning fleet-wide to save on capacity costs. What would you want validated before approving this at fleet scale?**

Model answer: Thin provisioning's capacity savings come from allocating storage on-demand rather than reserving it upfront, which means the aggregate provisioned capacity across the fleet can legitimately exceed actual physical capacity — the risk this introduces is that if actual usage across enough volumes grows faster than anticipated, the underlying pool can be exhausted with very little warning, and unlike a traditional thick-provisioned volume running out of its own space, thin-pool exhaustion can affect every volume sharing that pool simultaneously, not just the one that grew unexpectedly. Before approving fleet-wide, I'd want capacity-growth monitoring and alerting on the pool itself (not just individual volumes) with enough lead time to react, a tested procedure for what actually happens operationally when a pool nears exhaustion, and a clear understanding of which workloads are risk-tolerant enough for the shared-pool exposure versus which need dedicated, thick-provisioned capacity regardless of the cost savings. The capacity savings are real, but they convert a per-volume capacity risk into a shared, correlated fleet-wide risk, and that trade needs to be made deliberately, not discovered during an incident.

What this probes for: Recognizing that thin provisioning converts independent per-volume risk into correlated shared risk, and insisting on monitoring/procedure validation before a cost-driven architecture change at scale (§74, §107).

## 2. Patch, Upgrade, and Fleet Management Strategy

**Q: Design a patch management strategy for a mixed Debian/RHEL fleet operating under a regulatory requirement that critical security patches be applied within a fixed number of days of release.**

Model answer: I'd start from the regulatory timeline as the binding constraint and design backward from it, rather than bolting compliance onto an existing ad-hoc process. That means: automated vulnerability scanning and patch-availability detection across both package ecosystems (§94) feeding a single fleet-wide inventory, so "is this CVE patched everywhere it needs to be" is answerable without manual cross-referencing between `apt` and `dnf` ecosystems separately. I'd use a staged rollout — canary or a small validation ring first, full fleet within the remaining window — sized so the validation stage still comfortably completes inside the regulatory deadline even if it surfaces a problem requiring a fix-and-retry cycle. Given the mixed distribution families, I'd expect genuinely different patch cadences and package-naming/versioning conventions between the two, so the compliance tracking layer needs to normalize "is this specific CVE remediated" across both rather than assuming parity in release timing. Finally, every stage — scan, validate, deploy, confirm — needs to produce retained, auditable evidence, since the regulatory requirement isn't just "patch fast" but "prove you patched within the window," directly reflecting the audit-evidence discipline from §110.

What this probes for: Designing the process backward from the compliance deadline, correctly anticipating that mixed distro families complicate a unified patch-timeline view, and building in audit evidence rather than treating it as an afterthought (§94, §103, §108).

**Q: How would you evaluate whether to adopt immutable infrastructure for an existing large, mutable fleet, versus continuing to invest in configuration-management convergence for it?**

Model answer: I'd frame this as a migration-cost-versus-ongoing-risk trade-off rather than a binary "immutable is better" decision. The case for migrating strengthens with the proportion of the fleet that's genuinely stateless (or has already externalized state to managed services), since immutable infrastructure's core benefit — eliminating configuration drift by replacing rather than patching — only pays off cleanly there; a fleet full of long-lived stateful hosts would need substantial re-architecture before immutability is even viable, and that re-architecture cost has to be weighed honestly against the drift risk it removes. I'd also assess how frequently the fleet currently experiences drift-caused incidents versus how disruptive full-image-rebuild-and-replace pipelines would be to the team's existing change velocity, since immutable infrastructure trades fast, targeted configuration changes for a slower but more consistent build-and-redeploy cycle. A realistic middle path for a large existing mutable fleet is often incremental — starting immutable adoption with the most stateless, most drift-prone service tier first, proving the pipeline there, and expanding rather than attempting a wholesale fleet conversion in one step.

What this probes for: Evaluating adoption as a proportional, statefulness-dependent migration decision rather than a universal recommendation, and proposing incremental rollout as realistic architect-level judgment (§98, §102).

**Q: A cost-optimization initiative proposes aggressively right-sizing every instance in the fleet based on average utilization. What's the architectural risk in optimizing purely for average utilization, and how would you correct for it?**

Model answer: Average utilization hides variance — a fleet right-sized to its average load has, by definition, roughly half its capacity-relevant time above that average, and right-sizing tightly to the average leaves no headroom for legitimate peak load, failover absorption (if one node in an HA pair fails, survivors need spare capacity to absorb its load), or unexpected demand spikes. The correction is right-sizing against a percentile of peak load relevant to the actual failure and demand model — accounting for expected peak traffic patterns and, critically, the additional capacity needed to absorb a node failure in an HA configuration (§105) without a cascading overload of the survivors — rather than the simple average. I'd also distinguish workloads where brief performance degradation under a rare spike is acceptable (a candidate for tighter, average-driven sizing and real savings) from workloads where that degradation is unacceptable (which need deliberate headroom built in regardless of the average-utilization savings it forgoes), rather than applying one right-sizing formula fleet-wide.

What this probes for: Recognizing that average-based right-sizing ignores variance and failover headroom, and connecting cost optimization explicitly back to HA capacity planning rather than treating them as unrelated initiatives (§105, §107, §109).

## 3. High Availability and Disaster Recovery Design

**Q: Design a disaster recovery strategy given a stated RTO of 15 minutes, an RPO of 5 minutes, and a fixed, constrained budget. How do the RTO/RPO numbers actually shape the architecture, and where does the budget constraint force a real compromise?**

Model answer: An RPO of 5 minutes rules out backup-based recovery approaches outright — restoring from even a recent backup typically implies data loss measured in hours, not minutes — so the architecture needs continuous or near-continuous replication to a standby target instead, with replication lag itself monitored as a leading indicator of RPO risk. An RTO of 15 minutes similarly rules out a cold-standby approach requiring manual provisioning after a disaster is declared — 15 minutes needs a warm or hot standby already running (or capable of near-instant activation) with automated or scripted failover, since manual, human-driven recovery steps alone are unlikely to reliably complete that fast under incident-time pressure. The budget constraint is where the real compromise lands: a fully hot, always-on standby matching production capacity everywhere is the most reliable way to hit both numbers, but it roughly doubles ongoing infrastructure cost; a constrained budget likely forces choices like a warm standby at reduced capacity (accepting some performance degradation immediately post-failover while capacity scales up) or narrowing the fully-replicated scope to only the most critical services, with less critical services accepting a longer RTO/RPO than the headline numbers. I'd make sure that narrowing is an explicit, documented decision agreed with stakeholders, not a silent gap discovered during an actual disaster.

What this probes for: Correctly deriving architectural requirements (replication cadence, standby readiness) directly from the RTO/RPO numbers rather than treating them as abstract targets, and being explicit that a real budget constraint forces a real, named compromise rather than claiming to meet both numbers everywhere for free (§106).

**Q: How would you design monitoring and alerting for a Pacemaker/Corosync HA cluster such that a split-brain condition is caught before it causes data corruption, rather than after?**

Model answer: Split-brain occurs when cluster nodes lose the ability to communicate with each other but each concludes it should independently become active, risking both writing to shared resources simultaneously — the goal is detecting the precursor (loss of inter-node communication / quorum) rather than only detecting the resulting corruption after the fact. I'd monitor cluster communication health and quorum state directly (not just "is the service up," which can look healthy on both halves of a split cluster right up until corruption occurs), alert immediately on any quorum loss or fencing action regardless of whether the service currently looks healthy, and make sure fencing (STONITH) is actually configured and periodically tested rather than assumed to be working, since a split-brain scenario with non-functional fencing is precisely the case where monitoring detects the condition too late to matter. I'd treat a fencing event as always incident-worthy and requiring investigation, even when the cluster appears to have recovered cleanly on its own, since a self-resolved split-brain that isn't investigated can recur under the same conditions with worse timing.

What this probes for: Understanding that split-brain detection needs to target the cluster-communication/quorum layer proactively, and that STONITH/fencing needs active verification, not passive trust that it would work if needed (§105).

**Q: A disaster recovery plan replicates data to a secondary region purely for resilience, but the workload is subject to a data-residency requirement. How do you reconcile the two, and what does it cost the organization?**

Model answer: Data residency has to be treated as a hard constraint on every candidate DR target, not a separate concern optimized after resilience — any secondary region has to independently satisfy the same residency requirement as the primary, which likely limits DR options to other compliant regions within the same jurisdiction rather than whichever region offers the best technical DR characteristics globally. If the jurisdiction genuinely has only one viable region, the DR strategy has to shift toward intra-region resilience instead — multiple availability zones within that single compliant region — rather than cross-border replication that would improve availability while creating a compliance violation. The real cost is that this constraint can meaningfully cap how resilient the DR posture is technically able to become — a single-region jurisdiction with only two or three availability zones has a lower theoretical resilience ceiling than an organization free to replicate globally — and that ceiling needs to be communicated honestly to stakeholders as a consequence of the residency requirement, rather than presented as a DR design shortcoming.

What this probes for: Treating compliance constraints as binding architectural inputs that cap what's achievable, and being willing to name the resulting resilience ceiling honestly rather than overselling the DR plan (§104, §106, §110).

## 4. Security and Secrets Architecture

**Q: How would you design secrets handling across a set of services with genuinely varying sensitivity — some handling regulated customer data, others handling only internal, low-risk configuration?**

Model answer: I'd reject a single uniform secrets-handling policy applied identically everywhere, since over-engineering the low-sensitivity case wastes operational overhead and under-engineering the high-sensitivity case creates real regulatory and breach risk. For the high-sensitivity tier, I'd want centralized secrets management with strong access control, full audit logging of every secret access (tying back to §110's audit-trail requirements), automatic rotation, and encryption at rest and in transit as non-negotiable baseline requirements. For the low-sensitivity tier, a lighter-weight mechanism — still avoiding plaintext secrets committed to version control or baked into images, but without necessarily needing the full audit/rotation apparatus — is proportionate and keeps the heavier tooling's operational burden from being imposed where it isn't earning its cost. The architectural discipline is classifying secrets by sensitivity at the point of creation and routing each class to its appropriate handling tier automatically, mirroring the same tagging-and-routing pattern used for backup policy tiering, rather than relying on engineers to manually judge sensitivity correctly and consistently every time.

What this probes for: Proportional, tiered design rather than one-size-fits-all secrets policy, and connecting high-sensitivity handling explicitly to audit-trail requirements (§95, §110).

**Q: You're asked whether the organization should adopt SELinux enforcing mode fleet-wide, given that several teams currently run it in permissive mode (or disabled) because it "breaks things." How do you approach this organizationally, not just technically?**

Model answer: Permissive-mode-everywhere is a common and understandable local optimization — it removes a source of application breakage — but it's a real fleet-wide security regression if left as the default, since it means mandatory access control is effectively providing no enforcement anywhere it's disabled. I wouldn't approach this as a single fleet-wide flip to enforcing mode, since that risks breaking production services whose SELinux policies were never actually properly written or tested against enforcing mode. Instead, I'd treat it as a phased program: audit which services currently rely on permissive mode and why, generate and refine proper policies for each (using the AVC denial logs permissive mode itself produces as the input for policy refinement), and move services to enforcing mode one validated group at a time, tracking fleet-wide enforcing-mode coverage as an explicit metric reported to leadership rather than an invisible, team-by-team decision. The organizational piece is making the trade-off (short-term application breakage risk vs. long-term security posture) visible and owned at a level above individual teams, since left to individual teams, the path of least resistance will keep defaulting to permissive.

What this probes for: Recognizing that a technically-simple flip has real organizational and rollout dimensions, and using permissive-mode's own log output as the practical path to enforcing-mode adoption rather than treating it as an all-or-nothing switch (§91, §96).

## 5. Cost, Compliance, and Governance Trade-offs

**Q: How would you build a capacity-planning process that avoids both costly over-provisioning and risky under-provisioning across a fleet with genuinely unpredictable, spiky growth in some services and flat, predictable demand in others?**

Model answer: I'd reject a single capacity-planning cadence and buffer formula applied uniformly across the fleet, since a formula tuned for spiky growth will over-provision flat-demand services, and a formula tuned for flat demand will under-provision spiky ones right when it matters most. For predictable, flat-demand services, capacity planning can run on a longer cadence with tighter buffers, since the forecast itself is reliable. For spiky or fast-growing services, I'd favor either more frequent replanning cycles, elastic/auto-scaling capacity where the platform supports it so headroom is provisioned dynamically rather than pre-committed, or deliberately generous buffers justified by the cost of an under-provisioning incident in that specific service being worse than the cost of the excess capacity. The organizational piece is classifying each service's demand pattern explicitly and routing it to the appropriate planning cadence and buffer policy, and revisiting that classification periodically, since a service that was flat-demand a year ago may have become spiky as usage patterns evolved.

What this probes for: Recognizing that capacity planning, like backup and secrets policy, needs tiering by workload characteristic rather than one fleet-wide formula, and building in periodic reclassification (§107).

**Q: A regulated workload's compliance requirements and the organization's cost-optimization goals are pulling in opposite directions — for example, a requirement for redundant, geographically-separated logging infrastructure versus a push to consolidate logging into the cheapest single region. How do you resolve this?**

Model answer: I'd treat the compliance requirement as a non-negotiable floor rather than one input to be balanced evenly against cost — if geographic redundancy for audit-trail logging is genuinely mandated, consolidating into a single region to save cost isn't a legitimate optimization, it's a compliance violation dressed up as a cost decision, and I'd say so explicitly rather than quietly implementing the cheaper option. Where genuine cost optimization is still possible without violating the requirement, I'd look for it at the margins — cheaper storage tiers for older, less-frequently-accessed retained logs that still satisfy the durability and geographic requirements, or right-sizing the logging infrastructure's compute without touching its redundancy or retention properties. The organizational point worth making explicitly to stakeholders is that compliance requirements function as a floor, not a target to be traded off against cost like any other design parameter — conflating the two is exactly the kind of thing a compliance audit (§110) is designed to catch, at a much less convenient time than during design.

What this probes for: Correctly refusing to treat a hard compliance requirement as a soft trade-off parameter, while still finding legitimate optimization room elsewhere — architect-level judgment about which constraints are actually negotiable (§109, §110).

**Q: How would you decide, at a governance level, which teams are allowed to provision their own cloud infrastructure directly versus requiring changes to go through a centralized platform team?**

Model answer: I'd base this on the actual risk profile of what's being provisioned rather than a blanket policy either way — fully centralized provisioning for everything creates a bottleneck that slows every team down regardless of how low-risk their specific change is, while fully decentralized self-service for everything removes the consistency and guardrails that regulated or high-blast-radius infrastructure genuinely needs. A workable middle path is policy-as-code guardrails (constraining what any team can provision to pre-approved, compliant patterns — approved regions for data-residency reasons, mandatory tagging for cost allocation, mandatory encryption settings) combined with self-service within those guardrails, reserving centralized platform-team review specifically for infrastructure that falls outside the pre-approved patterns or carries genuinely elevated risk (handling regulated data, production-critical shared dependencies). This keeps low-risk, high-volume changes fast while keeping a human review gate specifically where the risk profile actually warrants it, rather than applying uniform friction everywhere or uniform freedom everywhere.

What this probes for: Designing a governance model proportional to risk rather than defaulting to either extreme, and operationalizing it via automated guardrails rather than relying purely on process and human review (§104, §110).

## 6. Key Takeaways

- Architect-tier questions consistently reward proportional, tiered thinking — backup policy, secrets handling, capacity planning, and provisioning governance all recur with the same underlying pattern: classify by risk/sensitivity/predictability first, then route to an appropriately-scoped policy, rather than applying one fleet-wide rule.
- A recurring discipline at this tier is treating hard constraints (data residency, regulatory patch timelines, compliance-mandated redundancy) as binding architectural inputs that cap what's achievable, and being willing to state that ceiling honestly to stakeholders rather than quietly working around it.
- Trade-offs at this tier are rarely resolved by picking one side — the strong answers name the specific compromise being made (budget vs. RTO/RPO, cost vs. compliance, provisioning speed vs. governance) and make it an explicit, owned decision rather than an invisible gap discovered later.
